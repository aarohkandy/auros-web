## The machines are not broken

A 2014 laptop opens a spreadsheet as fast as it did in 2014. Nothing about it wore out. What
happened is that the operating system it shipped with stopped being patched, and an unpatched
machine on a school network is a decision somebody has to defend.

So the machines sit in a cupboard. They are not broken. They are orphaned.

We build them an operating system. Not a general-purpose one with a store and a hundred
services you did not ask for. One that contains the applications your staff actually open, in
the language you actually teach in, with the lockdown you actually need, and nothing else at
all.

## Subtraction is the product

Most software is sold by addition. More features, more integrations, more surface. The thing
you are buying here is the opposite, and it is worth being precise about what it means.

A package that is not in your image is not disabled, not hidden behind a settings page, and not
sitting there waiting for somebody to find it. It has been removed from the image, so it is not
bytes your machines download, not a thing that can be exploited, and not an icon a Year 8 can
click.

The build reports back what it removed, by name, with the size of each. Not an estimate from
counting lines in a file. A measurement taken from the image you are about to receive.

Nobody else selling you a computer shows you what they took out.

## What doesn't come across

This is the part most vendors put on page four. It belongs at the top, because the organisations
that migrate successfully are the ones who knew this on day one, and the ones who leave in month
two are the ones who found out then.

Windows programs do not come across. Not one of them. Saved passwords in Chrome and Edge do not
come across, for a reason worth understanding rather than working around. Anything on a hardware
dongle does not. Anything built on Active Directory does not.

Your files do. Firefox comes across whole. Bookmarks, Wi-Fi, printers and the account name come
across, and the files are counted and hashed before a single byte of the old system is touched.

The full inventory, including how to test your own programs against the compatibility layer
before you commit anything, is on its own page and it is the page we would read first.

## Your data is copied before anything is written

The migration has an order to it and the order is not negotiable.

Your files are inventoried, copied to a disk that is not the system disk, and then verified
twice over: the file count has to match, and every file's hash has to match. Anything that
does not verify is re-copied once, and if it still does not, it is put on a list and shown to
you by name. **Nothing is written to your old disk until that list is empty.** If you would
rather stop, the migration stops and changes nothing. Windows still boots. You try again
tomorrow or you walk away.

We are specific about this because the simpler promise — *any single mismatch aborts the whole
thing* — is the one that sounds safer and is the one that fails. Files legitimately change
while they are being copied: OneDrive fetches something, antivirus touches a file, somebody
left a document open. A tool that gives up on all of it gets worked around, and a tool that
gets worked around is more dangerous than one that tells you precisely which four files it
could not read.

Only after that passes does anything get written to the machine. There is a moment, by design,
where your data exists in two places and the original disk has not been touched.

That archive stays on your premises. It is never uploaded, and we never receive it.

## We are replaceable on purpose

Every recipe is a public git repository, including yours. If we disappear, you clone it and
rebuild the same operating system with the same command we use.

That is not generosity and it is not marketing. A company that can hold your operating system
hostage eventually behaves like one, and a school cannot afford to find out which kind we are
in four years. So we removed the possibility and then we advertised it.

The instructions are written out in full, with the actual commands, on the replaceable page.
