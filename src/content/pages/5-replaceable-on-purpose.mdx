The normal end of a story like this is that the small vendor stops answering email, and a school
is left with two hundred machines running an operating system that nobody alive can rebuild.
Everything still works until the day it does not, and then there is no path forward that is not
a replacement programme.

This page used to answer that with a licence, and it is worth saying which one.

{/* auros-allow: names the withdrawn licence in order to record that it was withdrawn. Recording a right we took back is the opposite of offering it. */}
Until 2026-09-20 everything we wrote was Apache-2.0, and the answer on this page was *clone it
and rebuild it yourself, today, without asking*. We took that back. The code is ours and we want
it to stay ours, which is a commercial decision and not a noble one, and it is recorded in
`DECISIONS.md` as **D30** rather than left for you to infer from a `LICENSE` file.

So the answer has to be narrower, and it had better be exact.

## What you actually have, today

**The image.** It is installed on the laptops. It boots. It is a self-contained operating system
that does not call home to be allowed to start, does not check a licence server, and does not
stop working on a date. If we shut the doors this afternoon, every machine you have imaged comes
up tomorrow morning exactly as it did today.

That was never the risk, and answering *you have the image* does not answer the question you
asked.

**What you do not have is the maintenance.** The nightly rebuild against upstream is the product
— the spec says so in the first line, *the OS is not the product, the maintained image is* — and
a frozen image is a perfectly good operating system for about a month. Your real question is not
*do I have it*, it is *will it still be safe on my network in four years*.

## The commitment, in the words it will appear in the terms

> If Auros ceases operating, each customer receives the build files for their own image: their
> recipe, the base Containerfile, and the build scripts needed to rebuild and keep patching it.

Read what that is not, because the gap is the honest part.

- It is **not** a licence to our tooling — the compiler, the check matrix harness, the installer,
  the site. Those stay ours whatever happens.
- It is **not** redistribution rights. You can keep your own fleet alive. You cannot sell it.
- It is **not** in force today, because there is nothing to be in force about. It is a term that
  attaches to a customer, and there are no customers.

What it is: the specific artefacts needed to keep *your* machines patched, and nothing wider.
It costs us nothing while we exist, it is worth a great deal to somebody buying from a company
with no track record to check, and it hands a competitor nothing today — which was the entire
objection to the permissive licence in the first place.

## What the repositories being public does and does not mean

The repositories are public. You can read every line that goes into your image right now, from a
browser, without an account. That is deliberate and it is not going to change: a customer who
cannot read what is on their own machines is being asked for more trust than anybody should give
a company this size.

Public is not a licence. Everything in those repositories is all rights reserved. Reading is not
prevented; copying is not permitted. Both of those sentences are true at the same time and a site
that blurred them would be doing the thing this whole page exists to avoid.

There is one part we could not lock down even if we wanted to, and it is worth knowing: the image
we hand you contains GPL and LGPL software, because Fedora does. Those licences give the
*recipient* — you — rights we have no power to withhold, including the corresponding source for
those components. That is the ordinary position of every company shipping a Linux appliance, and
it is in your favour.

## What the handover looks like in practice

This is the one page on this site with a command line on it. Nobody using these machines should
ever need one. The person deciding whether to trust us with two hundred of them deserves to see
what the escape hatch actually consists of, rather than a sentence about our commitment to our
customers.

The files arrive laid out the way `auros-recipes` is laid out. From the top of that tree, on one
`x86_64` Linux machine with `podman` and a few tens of gigabytes free:

```
cd auros-recipes
podman build -t myschool:local -f customers/myschool/Containerfile .
```

That is your operating system, from your file, with the same packages removed. The generated
`Containerfile` is committed next to every recipe precisely so that nobody needs our compiler to
build it — that is `DECISIONS.md` **D28**, decided before any of this licensing argument started,
for this reason.

If our registry is gone too, the base is built first and the recipe pointed at the local copy.
`auros-base` sits beside `auros-recipes` in the same handover, so from the directory holding both:

```
podman build -t auros-base:hardened auros-base
podman build -t myschool:local \
  --build-arg BASE=localhost/auros-base:hardened \
  -f customers/myschool/Containerfile .
```

`BASE` is a build argument in every compiled Containerfile and its default is the base we publish.
Overriding it is the only way anything outside a recipe can change what an image is built on, and
it exists for exactly this paragraph.

Turning that into a bootable stick is one more command, and it is written out because two other
pages send you here for it:

```
mkdir -p out
podman run --rm --privileged \
  -v ./out:/output \
  -v /var/lib/containers/storage:/var/lib/containers/storage \
  quay.io/centos-bootc/bootc-image-builder:latest \
  --type iso localhost/myschool:local
```

Machines already running an Auros image do not need reimaging at all — push your build to any
registry you control, point them at it, and they take their updates from you with the same staging
and the same automatic rollback.

## The one thing that goes stale

The base image names a specific upstream image by its exact fingerprint, and the people who
publish that image delete old ones on a schedule — roughly three months, or sooner once enough
newer releases have shipped. Files that sat in a drawer for a year will name a foundation that no
longer exists, and the build will stop with a message saying the image is unknown.

The fix is to move the pin forward to a current upstream image and rebuild, which is a one-line
change and then the check matrix. We would rather write that down than let you discover it. A
rebuild instruction that works on the day it is published and fails silently two years later is
the failure mode this entire page exists to prevent, and pretending it does not apply to us would
be the same mistake in a different coat.

## What none of this gives you

- **Our test hardware, when we have any.** As of `2026-09-20` we have not imaged a single physical
  machine, and `hardware/compat.tsv` is a header row with nothing under it. Everything proven so
  far was proven in a virtual machine, which cannot tell you about your wireless chipset, your
  trackpad or your firmware.
- **Anybody to call.** What the paid tiers buy is not access to files. It is that somebody else
  watches the nightly build go red at six in the morning.

## What we have not done yet, which is the part to hold us to

There is a CI job in `auros-recipes` called `stranger`. It gets a container with `podman`, `git`
and nothing of ours — no toolchain, no account, no working tree — and runs the published command
verbatim, so that the claim on this page is executed rather than asserted.

Being exact about the state of it, because this is the paragraph where a vendor would round up:
**it is paused and it has never gone green.** It ran weekly while the Apache claim stood; D31 put
it on manual dispatch until there is a handover artefact for it to execute against, and the base
image it would need has not published once. The cron line is commented out in the file and the
footer of this page says `paused` rather than a time.

When there is a handover bundle, that job will build one from a cold container and this section
will carry a date and a run number. Until then what you have is a written term and a test that
exists and has not passed, which is less than we were claiming a week ago and is what we can
actually show you.
